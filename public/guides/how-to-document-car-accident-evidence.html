How to Document Car Accident Evidence: Photos, Voice Notes & Location

Photograph the vehicles where they stopped before anything is moved, then work outwards to the damage, the plates and the road. Record a voice note describing what happened while it is still fresh. Pin your exact location. Save your dashcam clip before it loops. Almost every dispute months later turns on evidence that took two minutes to capture, or was never captured at all.

Last updated 4 August 2026 · Applies to England, Scotland and Wales

Why the first ten minutes decide everything

Liability is usually argued long after the event, from whatever evidence exists. Two things degrade immediately, and neither can be recovered:

  • The scene itself. Once the vehicles move, their resting positions are gone. Debris is swept away, skid marks fade, and the traffic returns to normal.
  • Your memory. Recall of a sudden, stressful event degrades quickly and, worse, reshapes itself. An account given three weeks later is genuinely less reliable than one given at the roadside — even when you are being entirely honest.

Everything below is ordered by how quickly it disappears. Safety first, always: if the scene is dangerous, get clear of it before photographing anything.

Photographs: what to take, in order

  1. Wide shots, before anything moves

    Stand back far enough to show both vehicles in their final positions and the road around them — lane markings, junction, kerbs. Take these from two or three angles. This is the only irreplaceable photograph, so if you take nothing else, take this.

  2. Registration plates

    Every vehicle involved, clearly legible. This ties every other photograph to a specific vehicle and lets a DVLA check confirm the make, model, MOT and tax status.

  3. Damage, close up and in context

    Photograph each damaged area twice: once close enough to show the detail, once far enough back to show where on the vehicle it sits. Do this for both vehicles — photographing the other car's existing damage protects you against a repair bill that grows later.

  4. The road surface

    Skid marks, broken glass, fluid, debris fields and their position relative to the vehicles. These indicate speed and point of impact, and street cleaning removes them within days.

  5. Signs, markings and signals

    Give way lines, stop signs, speed limits, traffic light phasing, obscured or missing signs, overgrown hedges blocking sightlines. If a road defect such as a pothole contributed, photograph it with something for scale.

  6. Conditions and viewpoint

    Weather, light level, low sun, standing water. A photograph taken from the driver's seat showing what you could actually see is often more persuasive than any description.

Keep the originals. Phone photographs carry embedded date, time and often location data. Sending them through messaging apps frequently strips that and compresses the image. Share copies if you need to, but keep the originals untouched.

Your account: record it, don't rely on remembering

Write it down or, better, record a voice note — it is far quicker than typing with shaking hands, and it captures detail you would otherwise skip. Cover:

  • Date, time and where you were going
  • The road, direction of travel and which lane
  • Your speed, and the speed limit
  • What you first saw, and when
  • What you did — braking, steering, horn
  • The point of impact on each vehicle
  • What was said afterwards, as closely as you can recall
  • Anything unusual: a phone in the other driver's hand, an obscured sign, a vehicle pulling out

Stick to what you saw and did. Resist the urge to explain who was at fault — that is decided later on the evidence, and most UK motor policies contain a condition against admitting liability in any case.

Location: be precise

"The A34 near Newbury" is not a location. Record the road number and name, the direction you were travelling, the nearest junction or house number, and any landmark.

A what3words address identifies a three-metre square with three words, and according to what3words is accepted by the large majority of UK police, ambulance and fire services in their control rooms.

Give it alongside conventional details, not instead of them. what3words' own guidance is to gather as much location information as you can, in case a three word address cannot be accepted on that particular call. In an emergency, lead with the road and direction, then offer the three words.

Dashcam footage: save it now

Most dashcams record on a loop and overwrite the oldest footage when the card fills. Depending on card size and video quality, that can be a matter of hours. It is the most commonly lost piece of accident evidence, and it is lost through delay rather than accident.

  1. As soon as it is safe, press the lock or protect button if your camera has one
  2. If not, stop the camera recording so the loop cannot reach the clip
  3. Copy the file off the card onto your phone or a computer the same day
  4. Keep the footage either side of the impact — the approach often matters more than the collision
  5. Ask other drivers who stopped whether they have footage, and get their contact details

Nearby shops, homes and buses may also have cameras. CCTV is routinely overwritten within days, so ask immediately and in writing if you can.

Witnesses

A name and a mobile number is enough — do not try to take a statement. Independent witnesses carry disproportionate weight precisely because they have nothing at stake, and they disperse within minutes of the vehicles stopping.

Note where each witness was standing or driving, because what they could actually see determines how useful they are. Passengers in your own car are worth noting too, though an insurer will weigh them differently.

Mistakes that cost people claims

  • Moving the vehicles before any wide shot. Understandable, and irreversible.
  • Photographing only your own damage. You need theirs to stop the bill growing.
  • Close-ups with no context. A dented wing could be from anywhere.
  • Letting the dashcam keep running. The loop erases the evidence.
  • Not getting witness numbers. "Someone saw it" is worth nothing.
  • Waiting to write it down. The account you give in three weeks is not the one you would have given today.
  • Not telling your insurer because you are not claiming. GOV.UK says you must report the accident to your insurer regardless.

Read as a list, every one of those looks avoidable. They happen anyway, and they happen to careful people, because the moment you most need a methodical approach is the moment you are least capable of one. You have just been in a collision. The checklist you need is one you have never used before, and you are reading it for the first time in the rain.

In the days afterwards

  • See a GP or attend A&E if you have any symptoms — some injuries surface a day or two later, and a contemporaneous medical record matters.
  • Keep every receipt: recovery, storage, hire car, taxis, prescriptions.
  • Photograph bruising as it develops; it often looks worse on day three than on day one.
  • Date anything you remember later and keep it with the rest.
